Our story

A mouse, a boy in Calabria and a restaurant on Wells Street.

The name

Topo is Italian for mouse. Topo Gigio was the puppet who appeared on The Ed Sullivan Show in the 1960s, and a cartoon and comic-book character in Italy.

Frank, our founding owner, grew up watching Topo Gigio and his adventures. When he came to the United States from Calabria, he brought his family's recipes with him. They are on the menu today.

The cooking

We opened in 1988, in the heart of Old Town, with a modern Tuscan-style approach to Italian cooking. More than 35 years later the dining room is lively in the cold months and the garden is full in the summer.

The dishes people talk about are the mussels marinara, the Lake Superior whitefish, Conchiglie alla Sarda, Rigatoni al Filo di Fumo and the tiramisu, which is made fresh every day. Vic Damone cooked in our kitchen, and Linguine alla "Vic Damone" is his own recipe.

Pitch notes

What the current site is costing you

Everything here was checked on November 17, 2026, signed out, on a laptop and at phone width. Each item is something you can check yourself in a minute.

1
Every page tells Google not to list it.

All twelve pages of topogigiochicago.com carry a noindex, nofollow tag. That is usually WordPress's "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" box, left ticked. There are no page descriptions and no sitemap either.

In two web searches, one for the restaurant by name and address and one for "topogigiochicago.com dinner menu", your own pages did not come up. Menu-listing sites did. One of them shows Monday as open. Another shows Sunday hours as "9:00 PM to 4:00 PM".

Fix it today, whether or not you want a new site: in WordPress, go to Settings, then Reading, and untick that box.

2
On a phone, the home page is two columns of text about three words wide.

At a phone's width the welcome text, the story and the hours each run in a column 128 pixels across.

3
Some of the text is close to invisible.

"Hours", "Parking" and the note about holiday hours are dark grey on black, a contrast of 1.7 to 1. The notice that the garden takes no reservations is 2.8 to 1. The accessibility guideline asks for at least 4.5 to 1.

4
The banquet planner's email link is broken.

On the Private Banquet Hall page, clicking Elizabeth's email address opens a "page not found". The link is missing the "mailto:" that makes it an email link. That page is where a wedding or a company dinner starts.

5
The site contradicts itself about reservations.

The home page and the button in the header send people to OpenTable. The Contact page says "We do NOT accept online reservations".

6
The Reservations button carries a date from 2024.

The OpenTable link is fixed to October 15, 2024 at 7:00 pm.

7
A link on the home page opens a WordPress placeholder.

"We do offer gift cards … over the phone" links to a page that still has WordPress's sample text: "You might also try a plugin to add a contact form."

8
Hours are hard to find.

On a laptop they are inside a drop-down in the menu bar. On the home page they are in green italics near the bottom. Pinch-to-zoom is switched off on phones, so small text can't be enlarged.

9
Banquets, catering and gift cards all end in "email Vince" or "call Elizabeth".

None of them has a request form. The 26 professional photographs of the banquet room sit on a page Google is told to ignore.

10
The dietary icons are borrowed from other websites.

The gluten-free, vegetarian and pescatarian symbols on the menus load from Google's image cache, a stock-art site and a Dutch website. If any of those files moves, the symbols disappear from your menu.

11
Small errors a guest will notice.

"FIRST COME, FIRST SEVERE", "Baralo" and "Sanoma" on the wine list, "ADD A PROTIEN", "Paramgiaan cheese", "Parts Uknown", "DELIVERY UNAVALIBLE", a missing logo image on the Contact page, and The Knot and WeddingWire badges that link to those sites' front pages, not to your listings.

12
Your best food photographs are not on the site.

The site's media library holds professional photographs of the Conchiglie alla Sarda, a seafood risotto, a roast chicken, a pork chop, a chocolate cake and the dining room. No page shows them. The home page shows a screenshot of the conchiglie instead. This concept uses the professional one.

What already works

  • The menus are real text with prices, not PDFs.
  • OpenTable is set up and busy.
  • The banquet room photographs are professional.
  • The story, the awards and the policies are all written down. This concept reuses them.

What well-run restaurant sites do

I looked at Carbone, Gene & Georgetti, Italian Village, Bavette's, Gibsons Italia and Monteverde. These are the decisions they share.

1
One photograph of the place, the name, and how long it has been there.

Gene & Georgetti: "Chicago's original steakhouse. Since 1941." Italian Village: "Family owned since 1927." Bavette's shows one dim photograph of its dining room and the word "Chicago".

Here: the garden at dusk and "Italian cooking on Wells Street since 1988".

2
Reserve is always in the top right corner.

Carbone has "Reserve a table" there, Italian Village has "Reserve", and Gibsons Italia fixes a Reservations tab to the edge of the screen.

Here: Reserve in the header on every page, and at the bottom of the screen on a phone.

3
Private events have their own place in the main menu.

Carbone, Gene & Georgetti and Italian Village all give private dining a top-level link.

Here: Private events is the second link.

4
Hours and location are one click from anywhere.

Carbone calls it "Hours & Info". Gene & Georgetti calls it "Hours & Location".

Here: "Hours and location" in the header, and today's hours on the first screen.

5
Gift cards are in the main menu.

Italian Village, Monteverde and Gene & Georgetti all link them from the top of the page.

Here: Gift cards in the header.

6
A wordmark and one deep colour.

Carbone is navy. Italian Village is red. The photographs do the rest.

Here: burgundy, with white and a little ochre.

Why it looks like this

  • The colours are from your room. The burgundy is the velvet drapes upstairs. The ochre is the plaster walls. The green of the open-now light is the garden awning.
  • The type is a menu's type. Headings and dish names are in Besley, a Clarendon, the kind of letter trattoria menus and shop signs were printed in. The reading text is Libre Franklin.
  • The check is a tablecloth. The red and white band under the menu bar is the checked cloth of an Italian table, in the burgundy of the drapes upstairs.
  • Dots between the dish and the price, the way a printed menu sets them.
  • Your logo and the mouse are not in the concept. The logo would go straight back in on the real site. I left the character out of a demo because he belongs to someone else.
